Where Dickason stands

Alberto, in January 2022 Dickason made a decisive move. The staffing and temporary division, the business Don built from a Snelling franchise in 1984 and Martha expanded into dmDickason Temporaries in 1992, was sold to HireQuest Inc. That was roughly $18M in revenue walking out the door. What stayed was the PEO, the ASO, and the payroll operation. The business that Martha launched in 2002 as dmDickasonHR.

Four years later, the operation is finding its new shape. The sixty years of relationships, the local knowledge, the Southwest market presence (licensed in both Texas and New Mexico), those are real. Kelly Dickason is stepping into the Director of HR role, the next generation getting closer to the center of the business. The foundation is strong.

What is missing is the operational infrastructure to match. The post-HireQuest transition left Dickason running a focused PEO without the systems that a focused PEO needs. Compliance gets checked when someone remembers. Pricing stays where it landed years ago. Client profitability is a feeling, not a number.

This document is a structured look at the four areas where the operation is carrying risk or leaving money on the table, followed by the growth plan that turns those gaps into revenue.

A note on what we know: We have not been inside the operation yet. Everything in this review is based on what Alberto has shared and what we know about how PEOs at Dickason's size typically run. Some of it will be right. Some of it may be off. Stage 01 exists to confirm or correct every assumption before a dollar is spent fixing anything.

Your tagline

People. Power. Performance. Those three words are the right frame for this engagement. The people are here. The performance history is real. What we are building is the power, the operational engine that makes the other two compound instead of coast.

The operational review

Four areas where the operation is leaving money or carrying risk.

01
Compliance posture

A PEO handling co-employment in two states touches at least twenty regulatory categories. Worker classification, workers comp class codes, payroll tax deposits, 401(k) deposit timing, ACA reporting, I-9 documentation, ESAC accreditation requirements, state-by-state filings for TX and NM. Alberto indicated nobody has given the compliance picture a serious review in some time. For an Employee Leasing operation carrying co-employment exposure, that is a real gap. The penalties in this space do not give warnings. They arrive.

The Compliance Risk Inventory in Stage 02 maps every category and scores Dickason's current position in each one.

02
Pricing and the free-add-on problem

Premium services are going out the door at no charge. 401(k) administration, benefits broker work, workers comp coordination, all bundled as extras rather than priced as the real services they are. Each one has labor cost and liability attached. When they are absorbed into the base markup, the revenue gap compounds on every client, every pay cycle. The result is a book that looks full but runs thin.

The portfolio audit in Stage 02 shows exactly how much is walking out the door and where the markup should land.

03
The shape of the client book

Real Valley tells the story. Three worksite employees, $15 per payroll per employee, roughly $1,000 a year in revenue against $2,000 or more in service cost. Full 941 quarterly filings, tax reconciliation, ongoing payroll administration. Hours of skilled accounting time consumed every month for a client that loses money. Anthony Water Utility shows what a properly structured client looks like. The gap between those two is where the money is. The question: how many more Real Valleys are hiding in the book?

Stage 02 scores every client: keep, renegotiate, graduate, or watch.

04
Onboarding and the back-office workflow

Alberto flagged this specifically. After the HireQuest transition, the operation took on a new shape without building the systems to match. Onboarding drifts. Implementation timelines are unclear. The first six months of a co-employment relationship set the tone. When onboarding goes rough, clients quietly look elsewhere by year two. A clear playbook with real ownership, realistic timelines, and a 30/60/90 check after go-live is the floor for retention.

We map the current onboarding in Stage 01 and build the replacement playbook in Stage 03.

The growth plan

The review above identifies where money and risk sit today. The plan below shows how each one becomes a growth lever.

01
Revenue recovery through portfolio correction

The Real Valley math extrapolated across the full client book is the fastest path to revenue recovery. Every underpriced client identified in the portfolio audit gets a clear path: renegotiation with a new pricing tier, graduation to a service level that matches what they actually consume, or a managed transition off the book if the numbers cannot work.

The renegotiation roadmap assigns each client to a tier based on worksite employee count, service complexity, and current margin. Conversations happen on a schedule, not when someone remembers. The pricing framework gives the team a structure for every new proposal going forward, not just the legacy book.

Conservative estimate: 5 to 15 percent revenue lift from pricing corrections alone. That number comes from the known gap between what clients like Real Valley pay and what the service actually costs to deliver. The portfolio audit in Stage 02 turns that estimate into a specific dollar figure.

Revenue lever: pricing corrections across the existing book
02
Retention through operational excellence

Client retention in the PEO space is won or lost in the first eighteen months. The onboarding playbook we build in Stage 03 creates a repeatable process with clear ownership, defined milestones, and a 30/60/90 cadence that catches problems before they become departures. When onboarding is clean, year-two retention improves. When it is rough, the client starts shopping quietly.

Compliance confidence becomes a retention tool. Clients stay with a PEO they trust to keep them clean. When Dickason can show a client their compliance picture (workers comp class codes current, ACA filings on time, I-9 documentation complete), that is a concrete reason to renew. Not a pitch. A demonstration.

Service quality becomes measurable, not anecdotal. The database tracks onboarding completion rates, response times, compliance status per client. When a client asks how things are going, the answer is data, not a reassurance.

Retention lever: structured onboarding and compliance visibility
03
Competitive positioning for the next decade

Post-HireQuest, Dickason is a focused PEO. That is an advantage, not a limitation, if the infrastructure matches the focus. A company that does one thing well, with the systems to prove it, competes differently than a company that does one thing and hopes the relationships hold.

The database positions Dickason to compete with the tooling that national platforms take for granted. Client dashboards, compliance tracking, pipeline management, renewal intelligence. These are table stakes at Justworks and Rippling. They do not need to be table stakes at Dickason, but some version of them needs to exist for Dickason to hold and grow its position in the Southwest market.

"The Southwest's Premier PEO" backed by systems, not just history. Sixty years of relationships are the foundation. The database is what turns that foundation into a platform for the next sixty.

Positioning lever: infrastructure that matches the reputation

The bigger reason to do this now
