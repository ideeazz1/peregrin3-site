The leadership team is three generations of operational knowledge. Don Dickason built the staffing franchise starting in 1984. Martha Olguin-Dickason launched the PEO arm in 2002 and holds majority ownership. Kelly Dickason is Director of HR, the next generation moving closer to the center. Carol Salcedo, VP of HR and Operations, has held operational continuity through the divestiture. These are long-tenure relationships on both the leadership and client side, some running thirty-plus years.

What Don and Martha built is real. Sixty years in El Paso is not an accident. The challenge is not the relationships. The challenge is the operating infrastructure underneath them.

The post-2022 story

The divestiture was the right move. The transition is not yet complete.

In January 2022, Dickason sold its staffing and temporary placement division to HireQuest Inc. That was approximately $18 million in annual revenue walking out the door. What stayed: the PEO, the ASO, and the payroll operation. The part Martha built in 2002. The focused business Don and Martha had been running alongside the staffing arm for twenty years.

The move made strategic sense. A pure-play PEO is a different business than a staffing company that also does PEO. The revenue model is recurring. The client relationships are deeper. The compliance footprint is more complex and more defensible.

Three years later, the operating infrastructure has not fully caught up to the strategy. What was a division inside a larger business is now the whole business, and the systems, processes, and external presentation still reflect the staffing-and-PEO era in several places.

The signals are visible from outside. Here is what we found.

Eight things we noticed from the outside

Everything here comes from publicly available sources. Each one tells a story about where the operation stands today.

01
Two websites running at the same time

The legacy dmdickason.com and the current dmdickason.curreyadkins.com are both live. The legacy site still calls Dickason "the only Full Service Staffing Company in El Paso." That copy was accurate before Q1 2022. It is no longer accurate. Three years after the divestiture, the public-facing story has two contradictory versions running side by side.

What it signals: the post-2022 pivot is not yet reflected at the operating level
02
Two pricing tier vocabularies, both active

The new site offers Starter, Essential, and Supercharged packages. The legacy site offers Silver, Gold, and Platinum. Both are publicly accessible today. That means sales conversations, broker presentations, and client-facing collateral are almost certainly quoting different package names for the same services. Without a unified playbook, quoting is inconsistent across the team.

What it signals: internal alignment on pricing is incomplete
03
Staffing job listings still live on the legacy site

Active job postings for a Dental Assistant and a Cable Installer are still visible on the legacy site. Dickason no longer places workers in those roles. Candidates clicking those listings are either hitting a 404 or landing at a HireQuest-owned entity. Either outcome is leakage Dickason did not sign up for.

What it signals: the staffing-era digital infrastructure is still running unattended
04
Microsoft Dynamics GP named as the accounting backbone

A legacy CFO bio references Great Plains Dynamics as the core accounting system. Microsoft has been sunsetting that platform. The current state of the GP installation at Dickason is unknown from the outside, but any organization still running it is working against a forcing function. The migration is not a question of if. It is a question of when, and whether Dickason runs it on their timeline or on Microsoft's.

What it signals: a system transition is coming that will affect reporting and daily operations
05
Average client under 15 employees

The site self-describes the average PEO client as fewer than 15 employees. That is an operationally expensive profile. Onboarding, compliance management, 941 filings, W-2 production, and relationship maintenance carry mostly fixed costs per client. At low employee counts, those costs compress the margin significantly. The question is which clients in the current book are below the profitability threshold and at what rate.

What it signals: a portion of the client book is likely losing money at current rates
06
No system to score clients on profitability

Alberto confirmed this directly: no CRM, no view of which PEO plan each client is on, no expense-to-revenue calculation per client, no scoring of which services each client is consuming. The portfolio exists. The data to understand the portfolio does not. This is the central operational gap, and it is common in regional PEOs of this size and tenure.

What it signals: revenue leakage is invisible until it compounds to a crisis
07
Three inbound channels with no attribution

Dickason markets three inbound paths: Brokers and Agents, Refer a Client, and Direct. Without a CRM tracking channel attribution, there is no way to measure which channel drives the most revenue, which brokers are over- or under-compensated relative to the business they bring, or which referral relationships deserve more investment.

What it signals: some brokers are being overpaid; some referrals are being undercredited
08
Texas and New Mexico licensure only, with broader marketing language

Dickason markets its geographic reach with language that includes Arizona. PEO licensure, however, covers Texas and New Mexico. If a client expands into Arizona and needs PEO coverage there, Dickason cannot service that client through a licensed entity. Without a system tracking where clients actually have employees, that exposure is invisible until a client calls asking about a new Phoenix hire.

What it signals: successful clients growing out of the service footprint is both a risk and a growth decision

The math on one client

The floor of the profitability problem

One long-tenure client in the book. Three worksite employees. Charged at $15 per employee per payroll. Total annual revenue: roughly $1,000. Service consumed: full 941 quarterly filings, ongoing payroll administration, tax reconciliation, HR compliance overhead. Skilled accounting hours every month. Cost to serve: over $2,000 per year.

This client is not an outlier. It is the pattern that emerges when a book of long-tenure clients has not had a structured pricing review. Rates set five or ten years ago do not reflect what compliance and service complexity cost today. And no one renegotiates a relationship they value without a reason to.

The portfolio audit in Stage 02 applies this math to every client in the book. Even finding three more clients at this floor represents $12,000 per year in recurring margin, starting the day after the renegotiation conversation. The conservative portfolio-level estimate for pricing corrections: $100,000 to $500,000 in recurring annual revenue, depending on how many similar situations are in the book.

"We know about one client at that floor. The audit finds the rest. Even two more pays the engagement back in year one."
